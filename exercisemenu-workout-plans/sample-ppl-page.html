What Is a Push Pull Legs Split?

A push pull legs split (often called a PPL split) is a training structure that divides your workouts by movement pattern rather than individual muscles. Each session trains all the muscles involved in one type of movement, which means related muscles always work together and recover together.

Instead of a "chest day" where your triceps and shoulders get hit as secondary movers without dedicated work, a Push day trains chest, shoulders, and triceps together — the three muscle groups that work whenever you push something away from your body. This is biomechanically efficient: your shoulders and triceps are already warm and primed from pressing, so you get more out of their isolation work.

Why PPL works: The 6-day version hits every muscle group twice per week — the sweet spot for hypertrophy according to Schoenfeld et al.'s meta-analysis (2016), which found that training a muscle 2× per week produced significantly more growth than 1× per week.

The 6-day push pull legs program follows a repeating Push → Pull → Legs → Push → Pull → Legs → Rest cycle. Each pattern appears twice: once with a strength focus (heavier loads, lower reps) and once with a hypertrophy focus (moderate loads, higher reps). This undulating periodization approach makes the PPL training split one of the most effective routines for building both raw strength and muscle size simultaneously.

Who Should Follow This Push Pull Legs Program?

This push pull legs workout plan is designed for lifters who have at least 6–12 months of consistent training experience and can perform the core compound lifts (bench press, squat, deadlift, overhead press, barbell row) with proper form.

  • You can train 6 days per week and have 55–75 minutes per session
  • You've graduated from a beginner program (like Starting Strength, StrongLifts, or a full-body plan)
  • Your compound lifts have stalled on a 3-day program and you need more volume
  • You want to build both strength and muscle size
  • You have access to a fully equipped gym (barbell, dumbbells, cables, machines)
💡 Coach's Note

If you can only train 3–4 days per week, a push pull legs split isn't your best option — you'd only hit each muscle once per week. Consider our Upper Lower Split workout plan (4 days) or Full Body Workout Plan (3 days) instead. The PPL routine's real advantage only kicks in at 6 sessions per week.

Push Pull Legs Workout A — Strength Days

The "A" sessions in this push pull legs routine prioritize heavier loads (4–8 rep range) with longer rest periods (2.5–4 minutes). The goal is to build raw strength through progressive overload on compound movements. Compound lifts come first when your nervous system is fresh; isolation work follows to accumulate additional volume.

🔬 Rest Periods

A study by Schoenfeld et al. (2016) found that 3-minute rest periods led to greater strength gains on bench press and squat compared to 1-minute rests. For heavy compound lifts, longer rest allows better performance on subsequent sets. (Source: Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 30(7), 2016)

Push Pull Legs Workout B — Hypertrophy Days

The "B" sessions in this PPL workout plan use moderate loads (8–15 rep range) with shorter rest periods (60–120 seconds). The emphasis shifts from absolute strength to time under tension, metabolic stress, and accumulated volume — the three mechanisms of hypertrophy identified by Schoenfeld (2010). You'll use more isolation exercises and different angles to target muscles for maximum growth in ways the A days don't.

Progressive Overload System for PPL

Progressive overload is the #1 driver of long-term muscle growth in any push pull legs program. Without it, your body has no reason to adapt and build more muscle. This PPL workout plan uses a double progression model — you work within a rep range, and only add weight once you hit the top of that range on all sets.

The Rule: When you can complete all prescribed sets at the top of the rep range with proper form, increase the weight by the smallest increment available (typically 2.5 kg / 5 lbs for upper body, 5 kg / 10 lbs for lower body) at your next session.

How Double Progression Works — Example

Let's say you're doing Barbell Bench Press for 4 × 4–6 reps at 80 kg:

Week 1: 80 kg → 6, 5, 5, 4 reps

You didn't hit 6 reps on all sets, so stay at 80 kg next session.

Week 2: 80 kg → 6, 6, 5, 5 reps

Getting closer — still not 6 on all sets. Stay at 80 kg.

Week 3: 80 kg → 6, 6, 6, 6 reps

All 4 sets hit the top of the range. Time to increase.

Week 4: 82.5 kg → 5, 4, 4, 4 reps

New weight, reps drop back down. Now work your way back up to 6 across all sets.

Other Progression Methods

If you can't add weight (common on isolation exercises), use these alternatives:

  • Add reps — Go from 3 × 12 to 3 × 13, then 3 × 14, then increase weight and restart at 12.
  • Add a set — Go from 3 × 12 to 4 × 12, then drop back to 3 sets with heavier weight.
  • Improve tempo — Slow down the eccentric (lowering) phase from 1 second to 2–3 seconds.
  • Reduce rest — Same weight and reps but with shorter rest shows improved conditioning.
💡 Tracking Tip

Log every workout. Write down the exercise, weight, and reps for every set. You can't progressively overload if you don't know what you did last week. Use a notebook, a notes app, or a training app — whatever you'll actually use consistently.

Deload Protocol for Your PPL Routine

A deload is a planned reduction in training intensity and/or volume that allows your body to fully recover, repair tissue, and come back stronger. This push pull legs workout plan runs in 4-week blocks: 3 weeks of hard training followed by 1 deload week.

🔄 Deload Schedule

Weeks 1–3: Train as prescribed (progressive overload) → Week 4: Deload

Weeks 5–7: Train hard again (with new PRs) → Week 8: Deload or test maxes

During deload week: Same exercises, same schedule, but reduce working weight by 40–50% and reduce sets by 1–2 per exercise. Keep the reps the same. This maintains movement patterns while dramatically reducing fatigue.

🔬 Why Deloads Work

Fitness adaptation follows the General Adaptation Syndrome (Hans Selye, 1950): stress → fatigue → recovery → supercompensation. Without planned recovery, you accumulate fatigue faster than you adapt, leading to stalled progress or overtraining. A deload dissipates accumulated fatigue while preserving the fitness you've built, setting you up for new PRs in the next training block.

Warm-Up & Cool-Down Protocol

Pre-Workout Warm-Up (10 minutes)

Never skip the warm-up. Cold muscles are weaker and more injury-prone. Here's a three-phase warm-up that takes about 10 minutes:

  • Phase 1 — General (3 min): Light cardio (rowing machine, bike, or brisk walking) to raise core temperature and increase blood flow.
  • Phase 2 — Dynamic Stretching (4 min): Arm circles, leg swings, hip circles, band pull-aparts, bodyweight squats. Move through full ranges of motion — never hold static stretches before lifting.
  • Phase 3 — Specific (3 min): 2–3 warm-up sets of your first exercise with ascending weight. Example for bench press: bar × 10, 50% × 6, 75% × 3. This "ramps" your nervous system for heavy loads.

Post-Workout Cool-Down (5 minutes)

Light static stretching for the muscles you trained — hold each stretch 30–60 seconds. This won't prevent soreness, but it can help maintain flexibility over time.

🔬 Protein Research

A meta-analysis by Morton et al. (2018) in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that protein supplementation significantly increased muscle mass gains during resistance training, with a recommended intake of 1.6 g/kg/day for most people. Intakes above 2.2 g/kg/day showed no additional benefit. Spread your protein across 3–5 meals for optimal muscle protein synthesis.

Meal Timing Around Training

  • Pre-workout (1–2 hours before): A balanced meal with protein + carbs. Example: chicken breast with rice, or oatmeal with protein powder.
  • Post-workout (within 2 hours): Another protein-rich meal. The "anabolic window" is real but wider than people think — within 2 hours is fine, not 30 minutes.
  • Before bed: Casein protein or a slow-digesting protein source (cottage cheese, Greek yogurt) supports overnight recovery.

8 Common Push Pull Legs Mistakes to Avoid

1. Ego Lifting

Lifting too heavy with bad form builds injuries, not muscle. Use a weight you can control through a full range of motion. If you have to swing, jerk, or bounce the weight, it's too heavy.

2. Skipping Leg Day

We know. Leg day is hard. But your quads and glutes are the largest muscles in your body — they produce the most growth hormone when trained hard. Skipping them limits your whole-body growth potential.

3. Too Much Volume

More sets aren't always better. Pelland et al.'s 2025 dose-response analysis found that beyond ~20 sets per muscle per week, additional volume shows diminishing returns. Stick to the program as written and add sets only when you're recovering well.

4. Neglecting Rear Delts and Rotator Cuff

All that pressing without pulling creates internal rotation and shoulder problems. This program includes face pulls on both Pull days for a reason — don't skip them.

5. Not Tracking Workouts

If you don't know what you lifted last week, you can't progressively overload this week. Log everything: exercise, weight, reps, and how it felt (RPE). This data drives your progress.

6. Ignoring Recovery

Muscle grows during rest, not during training. Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep, eat enough protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg), stay hydrated, and take your deload weeks seriously.

7. Changing the Program Every Week

Program hopping is one of the biggest progress killers. Run this plan for the full 8 weeks before making changes. Consistency + progressive overload beats novelty every time.

8. Doing Cardio Before Lifting

Long cardio sessions before weights deplete glycogen and reduce your strength output. If you want to do cardio, do it after lifting or on rest days. A light 5-minute warm-up is fine — a 30-minute run before squats is not.

Push Pull Legs Workout Plan FAQ

We recommend at least 6 months of consistent training experience before starting a 6-day PPL. Beginners respond best to full-body programs 3× per week because they recover faster and don't need as much volume per muscle group. Once your compound lifts start stalling on a beginner program, that's when PPL becomes the logical next step. Check our Full Body Workout Plan for Beginners if you're just starting out.

It depends on how many days you can train. If you can commit to 6 days per week, PPL gives you more volume per muscle group and shorter, more focused sessions. If you can only train 4 days, Upper/Lower is the better choice — it still hits everything twice per week. Both splits are effective when followed consistently; the best split is the one you can actually stick to.

Strength days (A sessions) will take approximately 60–75 minutes because of the longer rest periods (3–4 minutes between heavy compound sets). Hypertrophy days (B sessions) will be shorter at 50–65 minutes due to shorter rests (60–120 seconds). If your workouts are consistently taking longer than 80 minutes, you're probably resting too long or doing extra exercises.

The program already includes core work on Legs B day (cable woodchops or hanging leg raises). Your core also gets significant indirect training from squats, deadlifts, overhead presses, and standing exercises. If you want more ab work, add 2–3 sets of planks or ab wheel rollouts at the end of any session — but don't overdo it. Your core needs recovery time too, and heavy compound lifts are already training it hard.

If you miss one day, simply pick up where you left off — don't try to combine two workouts into one. For example, if you miss Pull A on Tuesday, do Pull A on Wednesday, then continue with Legs A on Thursday. Your rest day shifts to accommodate the missed session. If you miss more than 2 consecutive days, restart the cycle from wherever makes sense, ensuring you're not doing Push and Pull on the same day.

Conventional deadlifts are placed on Pull A because they heavily involve the posterior chain (back, glutes, hamstrings). However, if deadlifts exhaust you to the point where your rowing performance suffers significantly, you can move them to Legs A day instead — just do them after squats. Another option is to replace conventional deadlifts with rack pulls, which reduce lower body fatigue while still training the back heavily.

With consistent training, proper nutrition, and adequate sleep, expect to see noticeable strength increases within the first 2–3 weeks. Visible muscle growth (hypertrophy) typically takes 6–8 weeks to become noticeable, and significant changes in physique take 3–6 months. Take progress photos every 4 weeks — the mirror lies, but photos side-by-side don't. Track your lifts too; if your weights are going up, muscle is being built whether you see it yet or not.
