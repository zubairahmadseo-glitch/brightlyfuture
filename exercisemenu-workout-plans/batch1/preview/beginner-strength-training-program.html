Quick answer

This beginner workout plan runs three full-body days a week for 12 weeks. Days 1 and 2 build strength with 3 sets of 5 on the squat, bench press, deadlift and overhead press. Day 3 uses lighter sets of 8. You add a small amount of weight to each main lift every week while form stays solid.

Day 3 squat uses about 85–90% of that week's Day 1 squat load for 3 sets of 8. It adds practice and volume without another heavy day.

How to Progress Every Week

Use one rule: if you completed all 3 sets of 5 with good form and at least 1–2 reps in reserve, add weight next week. This is linear progression, the most direct form of progressive overload.

WeekSquat (Day 1)Bench (Day 1)Result
360 kg × 5, 5, 540 kg × 5, 5, 5All reps, solid form
465 kg × 5, 5, 542.5 kg × 5, 5, 5All reps
570 kg × 5, 5, 445 kg × 5, 5, 5Squat missed one rep: repeat 70 kg
670 kg × 5, 5, 547.5 kg × 5, 5, 5Back on track

If you miss reps two weeks in a row at the same load, drop that lift by 10% and build back up with half-size jumps. The ACSM progression guidelines use 2–10% load increases, which matches 2.5–5 kg on most beginner lifts. Accessories use double progression: reach the top of the rep range on all sets, then add the next dumbbell or plate. More examples are in progressive overload for beginners.

Rest Periods and Warm-Ups

Rest about 2 minutes between main-lift sets and 60–90 seconds between accessory sets. A systematic review on rest intervals found that for untrained lifters, 60–120 second rests were enough to maximise strength gains; longer rests mattered more for trained lifters. If a set of 5 feels heavy, take up to 3 minutes.

Warm-up (8–10 minutes)

  1. 5 minutes of easy cardio (bike, rower or brisk walk).
  2. Bodyweight squats × 10, hip hinges × 10, arm circles × 10 each way.
  3. Ramp-up sets: empty bar × 8–10, then 2–3 sets of 3–5 reps working up to your work weight.

The warm-up set calculator works out the ramp-up loads for you. A standard men's Olympic barbell weighs 20 kg and a women's Olympic bar 15 kg; see how much a barbell weighs for other bars.

Why a Strength-First Plan for Beginners?

Getting stronger first lets you use heavier loads later in muscle-building training. The 2026 ACSM position stand found resistance training also improves physical function, and that strength improved most with heavier loads (80% of your one-rep max or more), 2–3 sets per exercise, lifts placed at the start of the session, and two or more sessions a week. This plan follows each of those points.

🔬 Beginners also build muscle

Training for strength builds muscle too. In a network meta-analysis, untrained participants showed greater muscle growth than trained ones, and growth was similar across load ranges. Heavy sets of 5 on the main lifts plus 8–12 rep accessories cover both goals.

If your main goal is muscle size rather than strength, our full body workout plan for beginners is the better starting point.

Who This Beginner Program Is For

  • You have lifted for less than six months, or not at all.
  • You have access to a barbell, squat rack, bench and a few dumbbells and machines.
  • You can train three non-consecutive days a week for 45–60 minutes.
  • You are healthy and free of injuries that limit squatting, hinging or pressing.

Not sure about sets and reps yet? The beginner sets and reps guide explains the basics behind this plan.

Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

  • Starting too heavy. Beginning light gives you many weeks of steady progress instead of stalling early.
  • Chasing failure. Grinding reps makes good form harder to keep. A meta-analysis found training to failure added little for muscle growth compared with stopping short.
  • Skipping the log. Write down every set. Our workout tracking guide shows how.
  • Adding extra days or exercises. More is not better in the first months. Recovery is when you adapt.
  • Ignoring pain. Normal muscle soreness fades in a few days. Sharp or joint pain means stop the lift and get it checked.

Eating for Strength Gains

Eat about 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight daily; muscle gains plateaued near 1.62 g/kg in a meta-analysis of 49 trials. Get enough total food to support training: if you want to gain weight, eat a little above maintenance; if you want to lose fat, a modest deficit usually still allows strength gains as a beginner. Use the protein calculator to set your target.

What to Do After 12 Weeks

Once weekly jumps stall even with smaller increments, you have outgrown simple linear progression. The next step is a program that mixes heavy, light and medium days and progresses more slowly, such as our 3-day full body workout for intermediates if you still train three days, or our 4-day upper lower split if you can add a day.

Beginner Workout Plan FAQ

How much weight should a beginner start with?

Start with a load you can lift for 5 reps with 3–4 reps left, often just the empty bar for presses and squats. Starting light gives more weeks of steady progress.

Is 3 days a week enough for a beginner?

Yes. Three full-body sessions train every pattern two or three times a week, which is plenty for strength and muscle in the first months of lifting.

Should beginners do cardio too?

Yes. Two or three sessions of 20–30 minutes of moderate cardio per week support heart health. Do them on rest days or after lifting, not before heavy squats.

Can women follow this beginner workout plan?

Yes. Women gain strength at similar or faster relative rates than men in the early months of training, and the same lifts and progression work.

What if I can't squat or deadlift with a barbell yet?

Use a goblet squat and dumbbell Romanian deadlift for the first 2–4 weeks, then move to the barbell once the movement feels natural.

When will I see results?

Strength usually rises within the first two weeks as your technique improves. Visible muscle changes often take 8–12 weeks of consistent training and eating.
