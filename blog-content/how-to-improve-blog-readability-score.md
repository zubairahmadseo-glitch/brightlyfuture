## What is a good readability score for a blog post?

A good readability score for most blogs is 60–70 on the Flesch Reading Ease scale, which matches a US grade 8–9 reading level. That band reads like plain English. A general audience can follow it on a phone without re-reading lines.

The Flesch Reading Ease formula looks at two things only: average words per sentence and average syllables per word. Higher scores mean easier text. The [Flesch–Kincaid tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesch%E2%80%93Kincaid_readability_tests) translate the same inputs into a school grade instead.

[IMG 02-readability-flesch-scale.webp | Flesch Reading Ease score bands and what each one means]

Your target depends on who reads you:

- **General blogs, how-to guides, product pages:** 60–70 (grade 8–9)
- **B2B, SaaS and technical tutorials:** 50–60 is fine, because readers expect terms like "API" or "canonical tag"
- **Content for beginners or non-native readers:** 70+ (grade 7 or lower)

Hemingway Editor [states that over half of US adults read at an 8th-grade level](https://hemingwayapp.com/articles/readability/readability-score). Writing above that level shrinks your audience before they reach your second paragraph.

## Does readability score affect Google rankings?

No. Readability score is not a direct Google ranking factor. Google's John Mueller [said in a 2018 Webmaster Hangout](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ranking-factors/reading-level/) that Google does not run basic algorithms that count words and syllables to judge pages.

The data backs him up. Portent [analysed 756,297 pieces of content across 30,000 search queries](https://portent.com/blog/content/study-how-content-readability-affects-seo-and-rankings.htm) and found no link between reading level and ranking position. The average Flesch score of the top 30 results sat between 51.8 and 53.1. Originality.ai reached the same conclusion in a [study of 13,582 top-20 results across 1,000 keywords](https://originality.ai/blog/best-readability-score-to-rank-in-google): pages that rank have similar readability, but the score itself does not predict position.

So why bother? Because readers decide whether your page did its job. That signal matters to Google even if the formula doesn't.

- **Readers scan first.** Nielsen Norman Group found that [79% of users scan web pages and only 16% read word by word](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/). In the same research, a version rewritten to be concise, scannable and objective scored 124% higher on usability.
- **Readability tracks with sales.** Portent's [study of 33 client websites](https://portent.com/blog/cro/study-the-readability-of-your-website-is-affecting-your-conversion-rates.htm) found readability explained around 11% of the variation in conversion rate, rising to about 13% for e-commerce. For B2B sites the link was not significant.

Treat the score as a diagnostic, not a target Google checks. It points to the sentences that make people leave.

## How do you improve your blog readability score step by step?

Edit in five passes, in this order, and rescore after passes 3 and 5. Doing them out of order wastes time, because splitting sentences often removes the hard words for you.

[IMG 07-readability-editing-workflow.webp | My 5-pass readability editing workflow]

### Pass 1: Score the draft and find the red sentences

Paste the whole post into a checker and note three numbers: Flesch score, grade level and the share of sentences over 20 words. The last number tells you more than the first two. In my competitor test, the two articles with the most long sentences (StanzaGo at 18% and Readable at 20%) had the most red highlights, even though Readable's overall score passed. A good average can hide a handful of monsters.

### Pass 2: Split every sentence over 30 words

Look for the natural break points: "and", "which", "while", "because" and any second comma. Each one usually hides a second idea. The opening sentence in my test had 36 words and three ideas. It became two sentences.

Don't turn every sentence into five words, though. Mix short punches with medium sentences of 15–20 words. Readers feel rhythm, and so do AI detectors, which I covered in [how to humanize AI content](https://brightlyfuture.co.uk/blog/how-to-humanize-ai-content/).

### Pass 3: Swap long words for short ones

Every three-syllable word pulls your score down. These swaps come up in almost every draft I edit:

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| utilise | use |
| approximately | about |
| demonstrate | show |
| facilitate | help |
| in order to | to |
| consequently | so |
| a wide variety of | many |
| is able to | can |

Keep a technical term when your reader needs it. "Schema markup" stayed in my rewrite because an SEO reader searches for that exact phrase. Explain it once, then move on.

### Pass 4: Switch passive sentences to active

Passive voice hides who does what: "the content that is being published" becomes "the content you publish". Yoast and AIOSEO both flag passive voice once it passes 10% of sentences. My original paragraph sat at 25%.

### Pass 5: Read it aloud and fix where you stumble

Formulas can't hear. If you run out of breath or lose your place, the sentence needs work, whatever the score says. This pass also catches the problem the next section covers.

Then fix the layout. Short paragraphs of two to four sentences, a descriptive subheading every 250–300 words, and lists for any three or more parallel items. None of these change your Flesch score, but they are what the 79% of scanners actually see.

## Can you get a readability score that is too high?

Yes. A score above 85 on an informational post usually means you cut detail, not clutter. Readability formulas only count sentence and word length, so choppy fragments score brilliantly while reading badly.

I proved it with the same paragraph. I chopped it into 16 fragments like "SEO is big. It has parts. Keywords count." The checker gave it a perfect 100.

[IMG 06-readability-tool-gamed.webp | Screenshot: a fragmented version scores 100 but loses the meaning]

That version tells the reader nothing useful. It dropped from 91 words to 35 and lost every explanation of why each factor matters. Yoast gave the same reason when it [replaced the Flesch score in its readability analysis in version 19.3](https://yoast.com/flesch-reading-ease-score/): the score "only looks at sentence and word length, ignoring context, tone, and audience."

My rule: stop editing for the score once you land in 60–70. After that, edit for meaning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the Flesch–Kincaid grade the same as Flesch Reading Ease?

No. Both use the same inputs, but Flesch Reading Ease gives a 0–100 score where higher is easier, while Flesch–Kincaid gives a US school grade where lower is easier. A 60–70 Reading Ease roughly equals grade 8–9.

### Why does my readability score change between tools?

Each tool counts syllables and splits sentences in its own way. Headings, list items and abbreviations like "e.g." are handled differently, so the same text can score several points apart. Judge progress with one tool only.

### Do headings and bullet points affect the Flesch score?

Many checkers, including ours, treat each heading or bullet as a short sentence, which pushes the score up. Paste body text only, or check with and without formatting, if you want a fair baseline.

### Does readability matter for AI search engines like ChatGPT and Perplexity?

Clear, self-contained sentences are easier for AI engines to quote. A direct one-sentence answer under each question-style heading gives Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT and Perplexity a clean passage to cite. No public study yet measures this with Flesch scores, so treat it as good practice rather than a proven factor.

### Which old posts should I fix for readability first?

Start with posts that already get impressions in Google Search Console but show short engagement time in GA4. People are finding them and leaving early, so clearer writing has the most room to help there.
