Why one ball is enough

It squats, presses, twists and throws. That last one is the thing dumbbells can’t do, and it matters more than you think.

4Workouts
8Illustrated moves
15–25Minutes
1Ball

A medicine ball looks like the simplest thing in any gym. That is its strength. One ball lets you squat, press, rotate, carry and throw, and throwing is something a dumbbell can’t safely give you.

Strength vs power

When you throw, you have to produce force fast. That quality is called power. Strength is how much force you can make; power is how quickly you can make it. Power is what lets you jump, catch yourself on a stumble or stand up from a low chair without thinking.

Research backs the ball as a power tool. In a 12-week study, young handball players who added medicine ball training gained more upper-body power than players who didn’t.1 Another study found six weeks of medicine ball work improved throwing speed in female handball players.2

Not only for athletes

A 2022 meta-analysis of older adults found that power training, lifting quickly and lowering slowly, improved muscle power and speed-based everyday tasks more than traditional strength training.3 That is why Workout 4 asks you to stand up and push the ball away quickly, then lower with control.

Rotation you can train

Most gym programs move forward and back. Woodchops, twists and side tosses train your trunk to turn and to resist turning, the way you do when you swing a bag into a car or pick up a child.

It fits the guidelines

The World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days a week for adults, and balance plus strength work on three or more days for older adults.4 The four workouts here cover both.

Before you buy

Choose your ball

Three types look alike but behave differently. Pick the wrong one for slams and it bounces straight back at you.

Medicine ballMost exercises

Rubber shell with a grippy surface. Bounces a little. Use it for squats, twists, chops and wall passes.

Slam ballSlams only

Thick shell filled with sand, so it lands dead without bouncing. The only safe choice for ball slams.

Wall ballCatching & throws

Larger and soft. Easy to catch, so it suits partner throws, kids and high wall throws.

What weight?
WhoSquats, twists, chopsThrows & passes

The speed test

Do 10 wall passes. If the last three are clearly slower than the first three, the ball is too heavy for throwing.

Buying just one?

A 3–4 kg rubber medicine ball covers every workout here except slams.

6 safety rules
  1. Slam only a slam ball. A rubber ball can bounce back into your face.
  2. Throw at brick or concrete. Not at drywall, glass or furniture.
  3. Clear 2 × 2 metres. Check for pets, toys and low lights before you start.
  4. Call “ball!” before a partner throw. Make eye contact, then throw.
  5. Kids train with an adult. The kids version is on page 10.
  6. Stop with sharp pain. Muscle burn is normal; joint pain is not.
!

Breathe out on every throw, slam and chop. Holding your breath while bracing hard can spike blood pressure. A sharp exhale also helps you move faster.

How to brace

Stand tall, then tighten your stomach as if someone is about to poke it. Keep that tension while you move. Your ribs stay stacked over your hips, and your lower back stays still while your hips and upper back do the turning.

Squat to press

Legs · glutes · shoulders8–12 reps
  • Hold the ball at your chest, elbows down.
  • Sit your hips back until thighs are near parallel.
  • Drive up and press the ball overhead in one move.
Tip: Breathe out as you press.
EasierSquat only, keep the ball at chest
HarderPause 2 s at the bottom
2{{FIG:slam}}

Ball slam

Full body · power8–10 reps
  • Reach the ball overhead and rise onto your toes.
  • Brace and throw it down just in front of your feet.
  • Squat to pick it up with a flat back.
Tip: Look at the spot you’ll slam, not at the ceiling.
EasierLighter ball, reset between reps
HarderSlam beside each foot, alternating
3{{FIG:twist}}

Russian twist

Obliques · abs12–16 touches
  • Sit tall, lean back slightly, heels on the floor.
  • Turn your ribcage, not only your arms.
  • Tap the ball beside each hip.
Tip: Your nose follows the ball. That keeps the turn in your upper back.
EasierHold the ball closer to your chest
HarderLift your feet off the floor
4{{FIG:woodchop}}

Woodchop

Obliques · shoulders · legs8–10 / side
  • Start with the ball above one shoulder, arms long.
  • Pull it diagonally to the opposite knee as you bend.
  • Pivot the back foot to protect your lower back.
Tip: Move like you’re chopping wood across your body.
EasierHalf range: shoulder to hip
HarderFast down, slow back up

More moves

5{{FIG:lunge}}

Rotational lunge

Legs · glutes · obliques8 / side
  • Step forward into a lunge, both knees near 90°.
  • Turn the ball over your front thigh.
  • Bring it back to centre and push back to standing.
Tip: Front knee stays over your ankle, not past your toes.
EasierSplit squat in place, no turn
HarderWalking lunges with the turn
6{{FIG:chest_pass}}

Wall chest pass

Chest · triceps · power10–15 reps
  • Stand 1–1.5 m from a solid wall, one foot forward.
  • Push the ball from your chest as fast as you can.
  • Catch with soft elbows and reload.
Tip: Aim for one spot on the wall at chest height.
EasierStep closer, lighter ball
HarderQuarter squat before each throw
7{{FIG:deadbug}}

Dead bug reach

Deep core8–10 slow reps
  • Lie on your back, ball over chest, knees at 90°.
  • Reach the ball back as one leg straightens.
  • Keep your lower back pressed into the floor.
Tip: Go slower than feels necessary: 3 s out, 3 s back.
EasierMove the ball only, legs still
HarderStraighten both legs
8{{FIG:sit_stand}}

Sit-to-stand

Legs · glutes · balance8–12 reps
  • Sit on the front half of a sturdy chair.
  • Lean forward and stand without using your hands.
  • Lower slowly, over 3 seconds, to touch the seat.
Tip: Push the floor away with your whole foot.
EasierHigher seat or add a cushion
HarderHover 2 s just above the seat

Coach Interview12
In conversation · Sadia Baloch

One ball, whole family

How our head coach uses a single medicine ball to train clients at home, whole families, and adults over 50.

Why a medicine ball for home training?

One piece of kit covers pushing, squatting, turning and throwing. It’s small and it doesn’t need a rack or a bench. Most people who train at home stop because setting up feels like a chore. A ball in the corner removes that excuse.

What can it do that dumbbells can’t?

You can throw it. Throwing teaches your body to produce force quickly, and that’s the part of fitness daily life asks for most: lifting a case into an overhead bin, or getting up from the floor after playing with the kids.

How do you get families training together?

Make it a game. Count passes, not reps. Kids love a target, so I tape a square on the wall and let them aim at it. Parents end up working harder than they planned because they’re competing with a nine-year-old.

What changes for clients over 50?

Lighter ball, more control, and a chair nearby. We keep the lifting phase quick and the lowering slow. Balance is built into every session; a 20-second heel-to-toe hold with a ball is harder than it sounds.

When do people notice a difference?

Most clients tell me everyday things feel easier first: carrying shopping, getting off the floor. The mirror takes longer, which is why the log on page 15 matters.

One tip for today?

Put the ball where you’ll see it. The easiest workout to start is the one already waiting in your living room.

The coach’s home setup

One 3–4 kg ball, one clear wall, one chair, a timer on your phone. Total floor space: about the size of a yoga mat and a half.

Families: start here

Do Workout 3 once a week on the weekend, then let kids pick one move from the cards for a 5-minute “bonus round.”

Technique14
Spot them early

6 form mistakes

Most medicine ball injuries come from a few repeat errors. Check yourself against this page in week 1.

✕
Twisting from the lower back

Turning only at the waist loads the lower spine.

Turn your ribcage and let your back foot pivot in chops and tosses.
✕
Rounded back on the pick-up

Grabbing the ball after a slam with a curved back.

Squat down to it, chest up, then stand.
✕
A slow, heavy throw

If the ball floats, you are training strength, not power.

Go lighter until every throw is fast.
✕
Holding your breath

Bracing without breathing spikes pressure and slows you down.

Breathe out sharply on each throw or slam.
✕
Arched back on core moves

During dead bugs, the lower back lifts off the floor.

Shorten the reach until your back stays flat.
✕
Knees caving in

In squats and lunges, knees drift toward each other.

Push knees out in line with your toes.
{{PICKUP}}
Picking the ball up

After every slam, and at the start of every set. Bend your knees, keep your chest up, and lift with your legs.

Quick Answers16

You asked

How often should I train?

Two or three times a week on non-consecutive days. Workout 4 works best three times a week.

No wall at home?

Pass with a partner, or lie on your back and press the ball straight up, catching it on the way down.

Will this help with fat loss?

Circuits raise your heart rate and build muscle, which helps. Fat loss depends mostly on your overall diet and daily activity. Reps and sets for fat loss

Is it safe for kids?

Supervised resistance training is considered safe for children when the load is light and technique comes first.5

My lower back aches after twists.

Stop twists for a week, then return with feet on the floor and a smaller range. If it continues, see a professional.

Can I combine this with gym training?

Yes. Use Workout 2 after a gym session, or Workout 1 on a day you can’t get to the gym.

Which workout should I start with?

New to training: Workout 2 at the beginner level for a week, then add Workout 1. Over 50: start with Workout 4.

Can I do two workouts in one day?

Yes, if they’re different. Workout 1 plus Workout 2 makes a 35-minute session; skip the core finisher slams.
