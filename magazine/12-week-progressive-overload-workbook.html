From the Coach

Now what?

Issue 01 gave you a starting point: the right reps, the right sets and a simple three-day plan. This issue answers the question that comes next. What do I do now?

The answer is progressive overload: doing a little more over time. Most people know the phrase. Far fewer have a plan for it, so they lift the same weights for months and wonder why nothing changes.

This workbook is that plan. Twelve weeks, three sessions a week, and one clear rule that tells you when to add weight. Every page is built to be used, not only read. Fill in the logs, tick off the weeks, and look back at week 1 when you reach week 12.

Print the log pages. Bring them to the gym. Write everything down.

01 · The ScienceProgressive Overload
Feature

Why your workouts stop working

Why progress stalls after a few good months, and the principle that gets it moving again.

Your first month of training feels like magic. The weights climb, your clothes fit differently and every session brings something new. Then, somewhere around week six or eight, it stalls. Same weights. Same reps. Same reflection in the mirror.

Nothing is wrong with you. Your body did what it was built to do: it adapted to the demand you gave it. Muscles grow and get stronger because training creates a stress they are not yet prepared for. Once they handle that stress comfortably, they have no reason to change further.

That is the whole idea behind progressive overload: gradually increasing the demand on your muscles so there is always a new reason to adapt. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) describes progression as necessary if you want your body to keep adapting.1

“Your body adapts to what you ask of it. Progressive overload means you never stop asking.”

More weight is not the only way

Most people hear “progressive overload” and think it means adding plates. Weight is one tool, not the only one. In a 2022 study published in PeerJ, trained lifters followed one of two plans for eight weeks. One group added weight and kept their reps the same. The other kept the weight the same and added reps. Both groups gained similar muscle size and strength.2

That finding shapes this workbook. You will progress by adding reps first, then weight, a method called double progression (page 6). It works with dumbbells at home, machines at a busy gym or a barbell, and it tells you exactly when to move up.

Effort is the part you cannot skip

Adding reps or weight only works if your sets are hard enough to matter. The 2026 ACSM guidelines note that light and heavy loads both build muscle, as long as each set is taken close to failure. You don’t need to reach failure; stopping a couple of reps short works.3 Stop too early and your log looks good on paper while your muscles barely notice.

So every session in this plan asks two things of you: do a little more than last time, and make each set count. The next pages show you how.

02 · 2026 UpdateNew Guidelines
What changed in 2026

The new rules of strength training

In 2026 the ACSM updated its resistance training guidelines for the first time in 17 years, drawing on 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 people. Here is what it means for you.

01Any training beats none

The biggest jump in results comes from going from no training to regular training. A plan you follow beats a perfect plan you skip.

02Train each muscle twice a week

At least two sessions per muscle group each week. This plan’s three full-body days train most major muscles three times.

0310+ weekly sets for growth

For muscle size, aim for at least 10 sets per muscle group per week. This plan works up to about 9–10 for most muscles.

04You don’t need to hit failure

Training to absolute failure isn’t required. Stopping a few reps short gives similar results with less fatigue.

05Light or heavy both build muscle

Loads from 30% to 100% of your max all work for growth, as long as each set is taken close to failure.

06Heavier for pure strength

For maximum strength, the guidelines favour heavier loads (80%+ of your one-rep max) on key lifts done early in the session.

In this workbook
How this plan follows the 2026 guidelines
  • Three sessions a week, so most muscles are trained 3×
  • About 9–10 weekly sets for most major muscles from week 4
  • Target of 1–3 reps in reserve, never grinding to failure
  • Big compound lifts first, while you are fresh
Source: ACSM Position Stand, Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults (2026) and the ACSM announcement. Summarised for general readers.

03 · Methods7 Ways to Progress
The toolkit

7 ways to progress, not just weight

Weight is one lever. Here are seven, with the right moment to pull each one.

1
Add weight

The classic. Use it once you hit the top of your rep range on every set.

Bench30 → 32.5 kg
2
Add reps

Same weight, one more rep. Your default move in this plan.

Squat3×8 → 3×9
3
Add a set

More total work. This plan adds sets at the start of weeks 4 and 8.

Row2 → 3 sets
4
Slow the lowering

A 3-second lowering phase adds time under tension. Useful when you lack heavier weights.

Tempo1 s → 3 s down
5
Increase range of motion

Deeper squats, a fuller stretch at the bottom. Build range before you add load.

SquatBox → full depth
6
Harder variation

Use it when the smallest weight jump available is too big for you.

Push-upKnees → toes
7
Cut rest time

Same work in less time. Better for fitness than for size, so use it sparingly.

Rest120 s → 90 s
Which lever, when?
Gym with full equipment

Use 1 and 2 (reps, then weight). Add 3 at the planned weeks.

Home, limited weights

Use 2, 4, 5 and 6 once your heaviest dumbbells stop being hard.

Short on time

Keep sets and reps; use 7 carefully, and never below 60 seconds on big lifts.

Change one thing at a time. If you add weight and cut rest in the same week and the session goes badly, you won’t know which change caused it.

04 · The MethodDouble Progression
The one rule

The double progression rule

One rule decides every progression in this plan. Learn it once and use it for years.

The rule

Work inside a rep range. When you hit the top of the range on every set with good form, add the smallest weight jump you can. Then build the reps back up.

Example: dumbbell bench press, 8–12 reps

3 mistakes that break the rule
✕ Counting ugly reps

Half-depth squats and bounced presses don’t count toward the top of the range.

✕ Jumping too far

Add the smallest weight available. Big jumps make reps crash and form slip.

✕ Adding weight everywhere

Each exercise moves on its own schedule. Squats may jump twice before curls jump once.

05 · Week 1Find Your Starting Weight
Before you start

Find your starting weight

No guesswork and no max testing. Use reps in reserve to pick the right weight in your first week.

Reps in reserve (RIR) is how many more clean reps you could have done when you stopped the set. It is the simplest way to measure effort. Research supports it for new and experienced lifters, and your accuracy improves with practice.4 Every set in this plan has an RIR target.

0FailureCouldn’t do another rep. Avoid in this plan.
1Near maxOne rep left. Last sets, weeks 9–12.
2HardTwo reps left. Your main target.
3SolidThree reps left. Weeks 1–3 while learning.
4+Too easyAdd weight on the next set.
The 3-step test
  1. Choose a weight you think you could lift about 12 times.
  2. Do 8 reps, then stop and ask: how many more could I do with clean form?
  3. Adjust with the table, then write the weight in your log.
Reps left after 8What to do
5 or moreToo light. Add 10–20% next set.
3–4Your starting weight for weeks 1–3.
1–2Good for later. For week 1, drop 5–10%.
0 / form brokeToo heavy. Drop 15–20%.
Already know a rep max?

Estimate your one-rep max with the Brzycki formula:
1RM = weight ÷ (1.0278 − 0.0278 × reps)
Start this plan at about 60–65% of that number.

Calibrate once a week

New lifters often stop sets further from failure than they think. Once a week, take the last set of one exercise to 1 RIR to check what “hard” feels like.

Small wins,

By ExerciseMenu EditorialInterview with our head coach

Starting is the easy part. Knowing what to do in week five, week eight and week twelve is harder. We asked our head coach how she takes a new client from their first week to their twelfth, and what she changes when progress slows.

Q: When a new client starts, what do you look at first?

A: Their habits, not their strength. I want to know how many days a week they can train without the rest of their life falling apart. After that, I watch them move: a squat, a push-up, a row. That tells me where to start far better than any test.

Q: What does the first month look like?

A: Boring, on purpose. We keep the weights manageable, the reps controlled, and I ask them to write everything down. The goal for month one is to own the movements. People are surprised how quickly they get stronger just by learning the lifts properly. A lot of that early progress is the nervous system getting better at the skill.

Q: How do you decide when a client should add weight?

A: When they hit the top of their rep range on every set and the last rep looks like the first. That’s the double progression rule in this workbook. I never add weight because a client feels good on one day. It has to show up in the log, not only in how they feel.

Q: What’s the biggest mistake you see in the first 12 weeks?

A: Adding weight too fast. Someone has a great week, jumps five kilos, their form falls apart and they stall or get hurt. Small jumps, often, win every time.

“Three sessions they show up for beat five sessions they skip.”

09 · ConversationContinued

Q: What do you change first when a client stops progressing?

A: I check sleep and food before I touch the program. Most stalls I see are recovery problems in disguise. If recovery is fine, I look at effort: are they stopping sets too early? Only then do I change the exercises or the rep range.

Q: Why is there a lighter week in the middle of the plan?

A: Fatigue builds quietly. By week six most people are carrying more of it than they realise. One easier week lets it clear, and clients often come back in week eight stronger than they left week six.

Q: What does a successful 12 weeks look like?

A: Thirty-six sessions logged, or close to it. Better form. Weights that were hard in week one feel manageable. The numbers matter, but consistency is the real result, because it carries into the next twelve weeks.

Q: One piece of advice for someone starting today?

A: Write it down. Every set, every session. Your log is your coach when I’m not there. When you feel stuck, open it and look at week one.

The coach’s 3 rules
  1. Add reps before you add weight.
  2. Fix recovery before you change the program.
  3. If it isn’t written down, it didn’t happen.

14 · Quick AnswersFAQ & Sources
You asked

Quick answers

Can I do this plan at home with only dumbbells?

Yes. Every exercise has a home swap on page 9. When your heaviest dumbbells get easy, use slower lowering, pauses or one-arm and one-leg versions.

The next dumbbell is too heavy. What now?

Stay at your current weight and go 2–3 reps past the top of the range, or slow the lowering to 3 seconds. Move up once you reach 15 clean reps.

Should I feel sore after every session?

No. Soreness is common in the first weeks and after new exercises, but it doesn’t measure progress. Your log does.

Can I add cardio?

Yes. Do hard cardio after lifting or on non-lifting days, and keep easy walks on rest days. Cardio before or after weights?

What do I do after week 12?

Run the plan again starting from your week 12 weights, and swap two or three exercises for close variations to keep it fresh.

I’m over 50. Is this plan for me?

The structure suits most healthy adults. Start at 3–4 RIR and progress reps before weight. Reps and sets over 50
